## 1. Dangers of climate change (Summary/Outline – Fließtext, keine Zitate, keine Zeilen)

In his speech at the UN climate conference in Paris in 2015, Barack Obama describes the dangers of climate change and asks world leaders to act together.

First, he explains that climate change could shape this century more than any other problem and that no country is safe from it. The earth is already getting warmer, because most of the warmest years on record have happened since 2000. In Alaska he has seen the effects himself: the sea is destroying villages and coasts, the frozen soil is melting, the tundra is burning and glaciers are melting very fast.

Then he warns about the future. Whole countries could disappear under water, cities could be abandoned and fields could stop producing food. This could lead to political problems and new conflicts, and many people would have to flee to other countries. Weak states and economies would not be able to cope with these problems, and the next generation would suffer most.

However, Obama also says that it is not too late if all nations act together now.

## 2. Analysis: language and communicative strategies (Name → Quote → Effect)

Obama uses many rhetorical devices to show how dangerous climate change is, to give his audience hope and to make them act.

| Mittel | Zitat (Zeile) | Wirkung (einfach) |
|---|---|---|
| Facts and numbers | “fourteen of the fifteen warmest years on record have occurred since the year 2000” (ll. 15–16) | The danger is real, not only an opinion. He seems serious and trustworthy. |
| Imagery / personification | “the sea is already swallowing villages” (ll. 22–23) | The sea is like a monster. The audience can imagine the danger. |
| Short sentences | “Submerged countries. Abandoned cities.” (ll. 30–31) | A dark picture of the future. They work like a warning and create fear. |
| Repetition | “Right here. Right now.” (ll. 39–40) | They must act immediately, and it gives hope: the future can still be changed. |
| Inclusive language | “we embrace our responsibility” (ll. 49–50) | He admits the USA's role. He seems credible, and the audience feels like one team. |
| Anaphora | “Here in Paris, let's …” (ll. 75, 78, 82, 91) | It shows a clear plan and calls the leaders to action. |
| Anecdote + tricolon | young Indonesian woman (ll. 106–116); “it wasn't about terrorism, it wasn't about the economy, it wasn't about human rights” (ll. 110–112) | Climate change is the most important topic for young people. This appeals to emotions. |
| Quotation (authority) | Martin Luther King: “there is such a thing as being too late” (ll. 125–126) | A famous name makes the warning stronger. Time is running out. |
| Rhetorical question | “can we imagine a more worthy reward than that?” (ll. 145–146) | The listeners think about their children's future and agree. |
| Contrast / antithesis + call to action | “A world that is marked not by conflict, but by cooperation” (ll. 153–154); “Let's get to work.” (l. 158) | A positive vision. The last words are a direct call to action. |

All in all, Obama first creates fear with facts and pictures of the dangers. Then he gives hope and finally calls on the leaders to act together. This makes his speech very convincing.

## Klausurfertige Kurzversion

- **Outline:** Climate change could shape the century. It is getting warmer (Alaska: sea, permafrost, glaciers). In the future: flooded countries, abandoned cities, no harvests, conflicts, refugees. The next generation suffers. But it is not too late.
- **Analysis:** facts → trust; imagery and short sentences → fear; repetition and inclusive “we” → hope and team spirit; anaphora “Here in Paris, let's” → plan and call to action; anecdote, quotation (MLK), rhetorical question → emotions; antithesis and “Let's get to work.” → positive vision and action.
